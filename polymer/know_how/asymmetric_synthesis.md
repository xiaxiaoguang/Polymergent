## Definition
Enantioselective (asymmetric) synthesis produces stereoisomeric products in unequal amounts, favoring one enantiomer or diastereomer. Enantiomers differ at every chiral center; diastereomers differ at one or more. Without asymmetric induction, reactions produce racemic mixtures in equal amounts.

## Why It Matters
Different enantiomers often have vastly different biological activity: L-aspartame tastes sweet, D-aspartame is tasteless; only S-(+)-citalopram is therapeutically active; D-penicillamine treats arthritis while L-penicillamine is toxic. Critical in pharmaceuticals and flavor/fragrance chemistry.

## Mechanism: Asymmetric Induction
Chiral features (substrate, reagent, catalyst, or environment) lower activation energy for one enantiomer over the other. Enantioselectivity depends on relative rates at the enantiodifferentiating step:

$$k_1/k_2 = 10^{\Delta \Delta G^* / (T \times 1.98 \times 2.3)}$$

Lower temperatures increase enantioselectivity; small energy-barrier differences produce noticeable effects.

## Methods
- **Enantioselective catalysis**: Chiral coordination complexes; privileged ligands (BINAP, BINOL, Salen, BOX) work across multiple reactions. Example: Noyori asymmetric hydrogenation with BINAP/Ru.
- **Chiral auxiliaries**: Stoichiometric use; enable diastereomeric separation by chromatography/crystallization; require attachment and removal steps.
- **Biocatalysis**: Enzymes or cells; very high enantiopurity, mild conditions, low environmental impact; requires screening.
- **Organocatalysis**: Chiral organic (non-metal) catalysts like proline; inexpensive, environmentally friendly.
- **Chiral pool synthesis**: Uses naturally occurring chiral building blocks (sugars, amino acids); restricts reaction scope.
