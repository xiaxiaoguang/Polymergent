## Definition & Mechanism

Vulcanization is the curing of elastomers by forming cross-links between polymer chain sections, increasing rigidity, durability, and altering mechanical/electrical properties. Generally irreversible. Cross-links consist of chains containing 1–several sulfur atoms that bridge allylic hydrogen atoms (C-H bonds adjacent to C=C).

## Vulcanization Methods

**Five curing systems in common use:**
- Sulfur systems (most common)
- Peroxides
- Metallic oxides
- Acetoxysilane
- Urethane crosslinkers

**Sulfur vulcanization:** Applied to polyisoprene (natural rubber) and styrene-butadiene rubber (SBR), typically used in vehicle tires. A "cure package" adjusts accelerators for specific substrate and application.

**Polychloroprene (neoprene):** Uses metal oxides (MgO, ZnO, sometimes Pb₃O₄) instead of sulfur. Ethylene thiourea (ETU) is the primary accelerator but classified as reprotoxic.

**Silicone vulcanization:** 
- RTV-1 (one-component): Hardens via atmospheric humidity, catalyst, and acetoxysilane, forming acetic acid. Shore hardness 18–60; elongation 150–700%. Excellent UV/weathering resistance.
- RTV-2 (two-component): Room-temperature curing to solid elastomer, gel, or foam. Flexible from −80 to 250 °C. Used for flexible moulds and electrical insulation (dielectric properties).

## Property Relationships

Short crosslinks → better heat resistance. Longer crosslinks → good dynamic/flexing properties but less heat resistance.

## Applications

Rubber hoses, shoe soles, toys, erasers, hockey pucks, shock absorbers, conveyor belts, vibration mounts, insulation, tires, bowling balls. Vulcanization greatly improves lifespan, function, and strength.
