## Definition & Chemistry

Polyurethane (PUR, PU) is a class of polymers composed of organic units joined by carbamate (urethane) links. Produced by reacting diisocyanates (or polymeric isocyanates) with polyols, often with catalysts (tertiary amines like DABCO, metallic soaps like dibutyltin dilaurate) or UV exposure. These are alternating copolymers with both components containing two or more functional groups per molecule.

## Key Properties & Structure

Properties depend heavily on isocyanate and polyol types:
- Long, flexible segments → soft, elastic polymer
- High crosslinking → tough or rigid polymers
- Long chains + low crosslinking → stretchy material
- Short chains + many crosslinks → hard polymer
- Long chains + intermediate crosslinking → suitable for foams

Polymer contains both urethane linkers and urea groups; urea forms separate "hard segment" phases affecting foam properties.

## Foam Types & Manufacturing

**Blowing agents** (water, CO₂, pentane, HFC-134a, HFC-245fa) create gas during polymerization. Water reacts with isocyanates to form CO₂ and amines.

- **Open-cell foams**: bubbles broken but edges retain shape; soft, air-permeable (cushions, mattresses)
- **Closed-cell foams**: bubbles intact; rigid thermal insulation
- **Microcellular foams**: mechanically frothed, no blowing agents; tough elastomers (steering wheels, shoe soles)

Properties controlled by blowing agent amount and surfactant additives.

## Applications & Production

Global production (2019): 25 million metric tonnes (~6% of all polymers). Foams account for 67% of polyurethane use. Applications include flexible/rigid foams, coatings, adhesives, fibers (spandex, PUL), automotive components (seating, panels, gaskets), thermal insulation.
