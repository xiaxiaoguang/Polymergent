## Definition & Mechanism

Cationic polymerization is chain-growth polymerization initiated by a cationic initiator that transfers charge to a monomer, making it reactive. Propagation occurs via head-to-tail addition of monomers to the growing carbenium ion chain. The counterion must be non-nucleophilic to prevent premature termination.

## Monomer Types

**Alkenes:** Limited to olefins with electron-donating substituents that stabilize the resulting cationic charge. Reactivity depends on ability to stabilize carbenium ions.

**Heterocyclic monomers:** Lactones, lactams, and cyclic amines. Reactivity inversely correlates with ring strain—larger rings (≥6 members) rarely polymerize due to low strain.

Monomers must form stable cations upon polymerization.

## Initiators

- **Protic acids** (phosphoric, sulfuric, triflic): Produce weakly nucleophilic counterions but yield only low molecular weight polymers.
- **Lewis acids** (SnCl₄, AlCl₃, BF₃, TiCl₄): Most common; faster with cation sources (water, alcohols, esters). Counterion less nucleophilic than protic acid systems.
- **Carbenium ion salts** (trityl, tropylium): For most reactive alkenes; give well-defined structures; useful for kinetic studies.
- **Ionizing radiation:** Forms radical-cation pairs; difficult to control.

## Reaction Conditions

**Temperature:** Overall activation energy E = E_i + E_p − E_t. When E is negative, lower temperatures increase propagation rate and chain length. Low temperatures (170–190 K) preferred for longer chains since E_t > E_i + E_p.

**Solvent & counterion:** Polar solvents increase free ion concentration and propagation rate. Smaller counterions (higher charge density) increase propagation rate; more easily solvated.

## Applications

Polyisobutylene (inner tubes), poly(N-vinylcarbazole) (PVK).
