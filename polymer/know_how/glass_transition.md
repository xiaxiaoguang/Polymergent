## Definition & Characteristics

Glass transition is a gradual, reversible transition in amorphous materials from hard, brittle glassy state to viscous/rubbery state with temperature change. Not a true phase transition—rather a kinetic phenomenon extending over a temperature range with material-dependent conventions (e.g., cooling rate of 20 K/min, viscosity threshold of 10¹² Pa·s).

Viscosity increases by ~17 orders of magnitude across ~500 K range without pronounced structural change. Differs from crystallization (first-order phase transition with discontinuous properties).

## Glass Transition Temperature (Tg)

Tg marks the range where transition occurs. Always lower than melting temperature Tm. Location depends on thermal history and cooling rate; slower cooling allows structural relaxation, yielding higher density glass and higher measured Tg.

Examples: polystyrene and poly(methyl methacrylate) ~100°C; hard plastics used *below* Tg (glassy state); rubber elastomers used *above* Tg (rubbery, flexible state).

## Physical Mechanism

Upon cooling, internal degrees of freedom successively fall out of equilibrium. Glass exists in kinetically locked state—entropy, density, and properties depend on thermal history. Time and temperature are partially interchangeable (time–temperature superposition principle).

Material exhibits smooth steps in thermal-expansion coefficient and specific heat through transition range. Below Tg, structure does not relax at cooling rate applied.

## Glass Forming Ability

Tendency to form glass during quenching depends on composition, predictable by rigidity theory. Many polymers easily form glasses without crystallization, even at slow cooling rates.
