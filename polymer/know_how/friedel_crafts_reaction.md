## Definition
Friedel–Crafts reactions attach substituents to aromatic rings via two main types: alkylation and acylation. Both proceed by electrophilic aromatic substitution.

## Alkylation
- **Agents:** alkenes (industrial scale), alkyl halides, enones, epoxides
- **Catalyst:** strong Lewis acid, typically AlCl₃
- **Mechanism:** For primary alkyl halides, a carbocation-like complex [R(+)---(X---AlCl₃)(–)] forms rather than a free carbocation; carbocation rearrangement typically occurs, yielding secondary or tertiary products
- **Limitations:** Product is more nucleophilic than reactant (alkyl groups activate the ring), causing overalkylation. Intermolecular reactions limited to tertiary or stabilized carbocation-generating agents; intramolecular useful only for 5-6 membered rings with primary halides
- **Reversibility:** Alkylations can be reversed; used in transalkylation and protecting group strategies
- **Industrial examples:** ethylbenzene from benzene + ethylene; cumene from benzene + propene using zeolite-based solid acid catalysts

## Acylation
- **Agents:** acyl chlorides, acid anhydrides, carboxylic acids
- **Catalyst:** AlCl₃ (stoichiometric amount required, not truly catalytic)
- **Advantages over alkylation:** No carbocation rearrangement (acylium ion stabilized by resonance); product ketone is electron-withdrawing and less reactive, preventing multiple acylations
- **Limitation:** Ketone product forms stable complex with AlCl₃, requiring stoichiometric quantities
- **Special case:** Gattermann–Koch reaction synthesizes benzaldehyde from benzene + CO + HCl using AlCl₃/CuCl mixture
- **Reduction:** Resulting ketones reduced via Clemmensen or Wolff–Kishner reduction to alkyl substituents without rearrangement
