## Definition & Process
Emulsion polymerization is radical polymerization using an emulsion of water, monomers, and surfactants (typically oil-in-water). Polymerization occurs in latex/colloid particles (~100 nm) that spontaneously form, not in monomer droplets. Surfactants or water-soluble polymers stabilize particles electrostatically or sterically to prevent coagulation.

## Smith-Ewart-Harkins Mechanism (Three Intervals)
**Interval 1:** Water-soluble initiator reacts with monomer in surfactant micelles (not monomer droplets, due to greater surface area). Growing polymer chain terminates; monomer-swollen micelle becomes a polymer particle.

**Interval 2:** Both monomer droplets and polymer particles coexist. Monomer diffuses from droplets to particles; more initiators react in particles.

**Interval 3:** Monomer droplets disappear; all remaining monomer resides in particles. Continuous slow addition of monomer/initiator can maintain levels as particles grow.

## Key Advantages
- High molecular weight polymers at fast polymerization rates (unlike bulk/solution methods)
- Viscosity remains near water-level, independent of molecular weight
- Excellent heat transfer via continuous water phase
- Final product often usable without further processing
- Low volatile organic compounds (VOCs)—preferred for adhesives, paints, paper/textile coatings

## Limitations
- Surfactants/adjuvants difficult to remove from final polymer
- Water removal energy-intensive for dry polymers
- High monomer conversion causes chain transfer to polymer
- Incompatible with condensation, ionic, or Ziegler-Natta polymerization
- Theory fails for water-soluble monomers (methyl methacrylate, vinyl acetate) where homogeneous nucleation occurs without micelles
