## Definition
Green chemistry is the design of chemical products and processes that reduce or eliminate the use and generation of hazardous substances. It integrates pollution-prevention and process-intensification approaches to improve resource efficiency and minimize waste and risk across the life cycle of chemicals and materials.

## The Twelve Principles (Anastas & Warner, 1998)
1. Prevention: Waste prevention is better than treatment/cleanup
2. Atom economy: Maximize incorporation of all materials into final product
3. Less hazardous syntheses: Avoid toxic substances
4. Safer chemical design: Achieve function with minimal toxicity
5. Safer solvents and auxiliaries: Minimize and use non-hazardous alternatives
6. Energy efficiency: Minimize energy; use ambient temperature/pressure
7. Renewable feedstocks: Prefer renewable over non-renewable materials
8. Reduce derivatives: Minimize unnecessary protecting groups and reagents
9. Catalysis: Use catalytic reagents (small quantities, reusable) over stoichiometric ones
10. Design for degradation: Products should break into non-harmful substances
11. Real-time analysis: Develop monitoring to prevent hazardous substance formation
12. Inherently safer chemistry: Choose substances/forms to minimize explosion, fire, release risks

## Green Solvents
Traditional solvents are often toxic or chlorinated. Green solvents are less harmful to health and environment. Ideally derived from renewable resources and biodegradable; however, biomass-derived solvents may be more environmentally harmful to manufacture than fossil fuel equivalents. Solvent fate after use (recycling vs. disposal) must be considered in environmental impact assessment.

## Quantification
Green chemistry greenness is being quantified by scoring systems incorporating chemical yield, reagent price, handling safety, energy profile, and workup ease. Example: nitrobenzene-to-aniline reduction scored 64/100 (acceptable); HMDS-based amide synthesis scored 32/100 (adequate).
