## Definition
A cross-link is a bond or short sequence of bonds connecting one polymer chain to another. Can be covalent or ionic bonds in synthetic or natural polymers (proteins, lignin).

## Synthetic Polymers & Material Properties
- **Cross-link density effect**: Low density increases viscosity; intermediate density creates elastomeric properties; very high density produces rigid/glassy materials.
- **Irreversibility**: Most chemical covalent cross-links are irreversible; materials degrade or burn when heated rather than melt.
- **Curing & Vulcanization**: Curing applies to thermosetting resins (unsaturated polyester, epoxy); vulcanization uses sulfur as crosslinking agent for rubber, producing rigid, durable materials (car/bike tires). Products cannot be easily recycled.
- **Thermoplastic elastomers**: Use reversible physical cross-links in microstructure; can be reformed by heat; used in snowmobile tracks, medical catheters.
- **Oil-based paint**: Alkyd enamels cure via oxidative crosslinking after air exposure.

## Physical Cross-links
Formed by weaker interactions: ionic bonds (sodium alginate + calcium ions), dynamic covalent bonding (polyvinyl alcohol + borax). Examples: gelatin, collagen, agarose, agar.

## Measuring Cross-link Density
Swelling tests: sample immersed in solvent; less swelling indicates more crosslinking. Calculations use Flory Interaction Parameter and Flory's Network Theory. ASTM standards: D2765 (mass change method), F2214 (volume change method).

## Biological Applications
- **Proteins**: Disulfide bonds and isopeptide bonds stabilize hair, wool, skin, cartilage. Permanent waves break/reform disulfide bonds. DNA crosslinks (cisplatin, mustard gas) interfere with transcription/replication—therapeutic for cancer but potentially lethal.
- **Research**: Artificial crosslinkers (dimethyl suberimidate, BS3, formaldehyde, EDC, SMCC) identify protein–protein interactions.
