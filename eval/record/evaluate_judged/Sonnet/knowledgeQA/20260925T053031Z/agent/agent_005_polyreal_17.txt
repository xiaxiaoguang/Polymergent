gold: The main challenge encountered in copolymerization reactions is that the copolymerization reactivity ratios (i.e., reaction activities) of functional monomers and conventional monomers often do not match. This is especially true for olefinic functional monomers with bulky functional groups, whose copolymerization reactivity ratios are usually much lower than those of conventional monomers. This mismatch leads to different consumption rates of the two monomers, with the more reactive monomer being consumed preferentially. As a result, the polymer chain segments formed in the early and late stages of polymerization differ significantly in composition, ultimately leading to an uneven distribution of functional groups throughout the polymer chain. This is mainly caused by the 'steric hindrance effect,' where the larger functional groups hinder the monomer from approaching the active center of chain growth, thereby reducing its reactivity. To improve the uniform distribution of functional groups, process techniques such as controlling the monomer concentration can be employed. For example, instead of using batch polymerization with a one-time feed, a 'semi-continuous' or 'continuous addition' method can be used, where the more reactive monomer (conventional monomer) is slowly added to the polymerization system. This maintains a relatively constant instantaneous concentration ratio of the two monomers throughout the reaction, resulting in a copolymer with a more uniform chemical composition.

## Answer

**The challenge – compositional drift / non‑uniform incorporation of the functional monomer**

When a functional monomer (especially one bearing a bulky pendant group) is copolymerized with a conventional monomer, the two monomers almost always have very different reactivity ratios (r₁ ≠ r₂, and the system is usually far from the azeotropic condition, i.e., not both r's <1 or both >1 with f₁ = f₁,az). Because the propagating radical/chain end reacts with the two monomers at different relative rates, the *instantaneous* copolymer composition (F₁, Mayo–Lewis equation) differs from the feed composition (f₁). As the reaction proceeds, the more reactive monomer is consumed faster, so the feed ratio f₁ drifts, and F₁ drifts with it. The practical consequence is:
- **compositional heterogeneity along the polymerization time-course** – chains (or chain segments) formed early are rich in one monomer, those formed late are rich in the other;
- **non‑uniform placement of the functional group** both between chains and along a given chain, instead of the statistically uniform (or designed) distribution desired.

**Why it is worse for bulky functional monomers**

A bulky substituent on the functional monomer creates steric hindrance at the propagating chain end, lowering the rate constant for its own addition (k₁₁ or k₂₂, depending on which chain end) much more than it affects cross‑propagation, or vice versa. This steric (and sometimes accompanying polar/resonance) effect pushes r₁ and r₂ far apart and away from unity, so no azeotropic composition exists in a usable feed range (or it lies at an extreme, impractical f₁). Consequently the two monomers are incorporated at very different rates, amplifying the drift problem described above.

**Process techniques to obtain a more uniform distribution of the functional group**

1. **Operate at (or near) the azeotropic feed composition** (f₁ = f₁,az from r₁, r₂) when such a point exists in a practical range — here F₁ = f₁ throughout the reaction, eliminating drift.
2. **Semi‑batch/“starved‑feed” (programmed monomer addition)** – continuously meter in the faster‑consumed monomer (calculated from the reactivity ratios and the Mayo–Lewis equation) so that the instantaneous feed ratio, and hence F₁, stays constant over the whole conversion range; this is the most common industrial solution when no convenient azeotrope exists.
3. **Limit conversion to low values** (then isolate/recharge) so that drift has not yet become significant, at the cost of monomer utilization and throughput.
4. **Use living/controlled polymerization (ATRP, RAFT, NMP, living anionic/ROMP)** combined with the feeding strategies above – because all chains initiate together and grow simultaneously at comparable rates, chain‑to‑chain heterogeneity from different reaction start/stop times is suppressed, giving a more uniform (or deliberately gradient/block) placement of the functional units.
5. **Reduce the effective steric bulk during copolymerization** by using a smaller “masked” precursor monomer (protecting‑group strategy) that copolymerizes with reactivity ratios close to the co‑monomer, then **deprotect/derivatize the bulky functional group after copolymerization** (post‑polymerization modification), avoiding the reactivity‑ratio mismatch altogether.
6. **Tune reactivity ratios via solvent, temperature, or monomer polarity/complexation** (e.g., use of Lewis acids, charge‑transfer complexes) to bring r₁ and r₂ closer to each other/unity, reducing drift.
