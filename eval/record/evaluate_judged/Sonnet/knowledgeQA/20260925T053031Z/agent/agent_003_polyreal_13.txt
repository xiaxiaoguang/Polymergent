## Reasoning

**Product A — mm‑sized porous chromatography resin beads → Suspension polymerization**
- Suspension polymerization disperses monomer (containing initiator + porogen/diluent) as droplets (0.1–5 mm) in a non‑solvent continuous phase (usually water) stabilized by suspending agents; each droplet polymerizes as a mini-bulk reactor.
- Droplet size is easily tuned by stirring speed/stabilizer to reach the mm range required for packed-bed chromatography media.
- Because polymerization occurs monomer-rich inside the droplet, adding a porogen (inert diluent) during polymerization creates permanent porosity/cross-linked networks — exactly what's needed for a porous separation medium.
- Good heat dissipation via the continuous aqueous phase and easy isolation (simple filtration) of large beads.

**Product B — micron-sized, highly monodisperse particles for instrument standards → Dispersion polymerization**
- Dispersion polymerization starts as a homogeneous solution (monomer + initiator + stabilizer in a solvent that dissolves monomer but not polymer); nucleation occurs once, early, in a short burst, followed by growth of all particles from that single nucleation event without significant new particle formation.
- This "single nucleation, uniform growth" mechanism intrinsically yields very narrow (near-monodisperse, PDI ~1.01–1.05) particles in the 0.5–10 µm range — precisely the size and uniformity needed for particle-size calibration/reference standards (e.g., for flow cytometry, microscopy, Coulter counters).
- Suspension polymerization cannot give this narrow size distribution (droplet break-up is stochastic), and emulsion polymerization particles are too small (nm-scale) for this application.

**Product C — nano-sized particles for latex paint → Emulsion polymerization**
- Emulsion polymerization (Smith–Ewart–Harkins mechanism) nucleates polymer particles inside surfactant micelles, producing colloidally stable latex particles in the ~50–500 nm range — matching the nanoscale requirement.
- The continuous aqueous phase keeps viscosity low regardless of the high molecular weight achieved, gives excellent heat transfer, and produces a water-borne, low-VOC product that can be used directly (as a latex) without further processing — ideal for paint/coating formulation.
- This is the industrially standard route for producing latex paints, adhesives, and coatings for exactly these reasons.
