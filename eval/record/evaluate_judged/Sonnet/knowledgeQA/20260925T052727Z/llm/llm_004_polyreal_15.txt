gold: The main advantages of the physical composite method are:
1. **Simple and quick process**: Compared to chemical methods that require complex chemical reactions, purification, and post-treatment, the preparation process of physical composites (such as solution blending, melt blending) is generally simpler, faster, and can be synchronized with material forming processes (such as extrusion, injection molding).
2. **High versatility**: This method imposes fewer constraints on the chemical structure of functional components and polymer matrices. It does not require reactive functional groups between the two, allowing for a wide range of polymer matrices to be selected, facilitating the use of the excellent properties of existing commercial polymers.

A major disadvantage of the physical composite method is the **low degree of bonding between functional components and the polymer matrix**. There are no strong chemical bonds between them, relying mainly on weak interactions such as van der Waals forces. This leads to instability in functional performance, as small molecular functional components can easily **leach or migrate** out of the polymer matrix during use (for example, being extracted by solvents or seeping to the material surface), resulting in a gradual decline or even loss of the material's functionality.

This makes the process easier to control, energy‑saving, and suitable for large‑scale, continuous industrial production.

2. **Broad material selection** – Since no covalent bonding or reactive functional groups are required, almost any polymer matrix can be combined with almost any functional additive (inorganic particles, pigments, drugs, flame retardants, conductive fillers, etc.), regardless of their chemical reactivity. This greatly expands the range of usable materials and allows easy adjustment of composition/ratio to tune properties.

3. **Preservation of original properties** – Because the components are not chemically altered, each retains its intrinsic physical/chemical/functional properties (e.g., crystallinity, optical, electrical, or biological activity), and the functional performance can often be directly predicted from the properties of the individual components.

4. **Low cost and process flexibility** – Fewer synthetic steps, no need for expensive coupling agents, and compatibility with conventional processing equipment (extrusion, casting, coating) lower production costs and shorten development cycles.

**Major disadvantage and its effect on stability:**

Because the functional component is only physically dispersed in the matrix (held by weak van der Waals forces or simple physical entrapment rather than strong covalent/chemical bonds), the interfacial interaction between the two phases is weak. This leads to poor compatibility, easy migration, leaching, aggregation, or phase separation of the functional additive over time or under environmental stresses (heat, solvent, mechanical stress, aging). As a result, the functional performance of the composite (e.g., conductivity, drug release, antibacterial activity, catalytic efficiency) tends to **degrade or become inconsistent over time**, showing poorer long‑term stability and durability compared with chemically bonded functional polymers.
