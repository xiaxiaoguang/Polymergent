gold: The main challenge encountered in copolymerization reactions is that the copolymerization reactivity ratios (i.e., reaction activities) of functional monomers and conventional monomers often do not match. This is especially true for olefinic functional monomers with bulky functional groups, whose copolymerization reactivity ratios are usually much lower than those of conventional monomers. This mismatch leads to different consumption rates of the two monomers, with the more reactive monomer being consumed preferentially. As a result, the polymer chain segments formed in the early and late stages of polymerization differ significantly in composition, ultimately leading to an uneven distribution of functional groups throughout the polymer chain. This is mainly caused by the 'steric hindrance effect,' where the larger functional groups hinder the monomer from approaching the active center of chain growth, thereby reducing its reactivity. To improve the uniform distribution of functional groups, process techniques such as controlling the monomer concentration can be employed. For example, instead of using batch polymerization with a one-time feed, a 'semi-continuous' or 'continuous addition' method can be used, where the more reactive monomer (conventional monomer) is slowly added to the polymerization system. This maintains a relatively constant instantaneous concentration ratio of the two monomers throughout the reaction, resulting in a copolymer with a more uniform chemical composition.
parsed: **The challenge:** When a bulky functional monomer (M2) is copolymerized with a conventional monomer (M1), the reactivity ratios r1 and r2 are usually very different from each other (and from 1), e.g. r1 >> 1, r2 << 1. This means the two monomers add to the growing chain at very different rates, so the instantaneous copolymer composition differs greatly from the feed composition. As conversion proceeds, the faster‑reacting monomer is depleted first and the feed (and hence the copolymer) composition drifts continuously. The result is a copolymer in which the functional‑group content varies both along a single chain (blocky/gradient placement rather than random) and from chain to chain (compositional heterogeneity), so the functional groups are not uniformly distributed in the product.

**Why it happens:** A bulky substituent on the functional monomer produces strong steric hindrance and sometimes electronic effects at the propagating radical/ion center, making the addition of that monomer to the growing chain (and/or the addition of the other monomer next to it) much slower or faster than the "ideal" random copolymerization case. This large disparity in relative reactivity (r1r2 far from 1) is the root cause of the composition drift.

**Ways to improve uniformity:**
1. **Controlled/"starved" continuous or semi‑continuous feeding** – instead of charging all monomers at once, the more reactive monomer (or the monomer pair) is fed slowly and continuously (or in small increments) at a rate matched to its consumption rate, keeping the instantaneous feed ratio (and thus copolymer composition) constant throughout the reaction (semi-batch/monomer-starved process).
2. **Feedback‑controlled addition** – monitor conversion/composition on-line (e.g., by NMR, IR, or mass balance) and adjust the addition rate of each monomer accordingly to maintain the desired composition.
3. **Pre-calculated programmed feed** – use the reactivity ratios (from the copolymerization equation) to calculate and program a feed profile that compensates for the differing consumption rates, so that the cumulative copolymer composition stays uniform.
4. **Low overall conversion with monomer recycling** – stop the reaction at low conversion (where composition drift is minimal) and recycle/reuse the unreacted monomer mixture (after replenishing the used monomer) to build up molecular weight while keeping composition uniform.
5. **Choice of polymerization technique/medium** – use techniques such as emulsion or solution polymerization with controlled monomer addition, or adjust temperature/solvent to bring r1 and r2 closer to unity, thereby reducing the inherent reactivity mismatch.

Using these process strategies—especially controlled or programmed semi-continuous monomer feeding—allows the copolymer composition (and hence the distribution of the bulky functional groups) to remain uniform throughout the polymerization, despite the unfavorable reactivity ratios.
