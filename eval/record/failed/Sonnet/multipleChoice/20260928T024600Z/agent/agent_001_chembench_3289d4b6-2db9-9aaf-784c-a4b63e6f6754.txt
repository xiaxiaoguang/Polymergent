**Answer: B. Ethylene**

Reasoning: Ethylene is the classic example that can be polymerized by two fundamentally different mechanisms:
- **Radical (free-radical) polymerization**: high-pressure process producing LDPE (branched, radical chain-growth mechanism)
- **Polyinsertion (coordination) polymerization**: Ziegler–Natta or Phillips catalysts producing HDPE/LLDPE (linear, coordination-insertion mechanism via metal center)

Propylene, in contrast, cannot be practically radical-polymerized to high molecular weight because the allylic C–H bonds on the methyl substituent cause severe degradative chain transfer, limiting radical polymerization to only very low-MW oligomers — so it is essentially restricted to coordination polymerization industrially. Isobutylene only undergoes cationic polymerization (steric/electronic stabilization of tertiary carbocation, but its 1,1-disubstitution prevents both radical and coordination insertion polymerization to high MW). Dienes (butadiene, isoprene) are indeed radically and anionically/coordination polymerized, but the textbook/canonical pairing of "radical vs. polyinsertion" duality is most cleanly and classically illustrated by ethylene (LDPE vs. HDPE), making it the intended answer.
